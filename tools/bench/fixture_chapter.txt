Chapter Seven: The Glass Orchard

The orchard had been glass for as long as anyone in Wrenmouth could remember, and on still mornings the trees rang faintly when the sun first struck them, a sound like a spoon drawn slowly around the rim of a cup. Ilse Marrow had grown up listening to it from the attic window. She’d never once thought it strange.

“You’re late,” said her grandmother, without looking up from the stove.

“I’m not late. The bell hasn’t gone yet.”

“The bell,” said her grandmother, “is a liar, and so are you.” But she was smiling when she said it, and she slid a plate of burnt toast across the table as though it were a peace offering, which, in that house, it usually was.

Ilse ate standing up. Outside, the light was turning from grey to the colour of weak tea, and somewhere beyond the hedge the first of the glass branches began to hum. She counted under her breath, the way she’d been taught: one, two, three… and on the fourth beat the whole orchard answered, a long low chord that made the windowpanes shiver in their frames.

“It’s louder today,” she said.

Her grandmother put down the spoon. For a moment neither of them spoke. Then the old woman wiped her hands on her apron, very slowly, and said, “Fetch your coat.”

They walked down through the wet grass together, past the woodpile and the broken cold frame and the row of beehives that hadn’t held bees since the winter Ilse turned nine, and the nearer they came to the orchard the stranger the air became, thick and bright and faintly sweet, like the inside of a jar of honey that had been left too long on a sunny sill, until at last they stood at the edge of the first terrace, where the oldest trees grew, and Ilse saw that one of them, the tallest, the one her grandfather had planted on the morning she was born, had cracked clean through from crown to root and was weeping a thin silver light onto the ground.

“Oh,” said Ilse. It was all she could manage.

“Don’t touch it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were. You always do.” Her grandmother crouched beside the trunk, her knees cracking, and held one hand an inch above the split, not quite touching it. “It’s warm,” she murmured. “It shouldn’t be warm.”

Ilse felt the hair rise along her arms. She had seen the orchard in every season, under frost and under rain and once, terrifyingly, under a sky full of green lightning, and not one tree had ever so much as chipped. Glass didn’t break here. That was the whole point. That was the promise the town had been built on.

“What does it mean?” she asked.

Her grandmother didn’t answer. Instead she stood, and brushed off her skirt, and looked up the hill toward the house with an expression Ilse had never seen on her before: not fear, exactly, but something older and more tired than fear, the look of someone who has been waiting a long time for a letter and has just heard the postman at the gate.

“Go and get Tomas,” she said. “Tell him to bring the ledger. The blue one, not the red. And Ilse… run.”

She ran.

Tomas Fell kept the lighthouse at the end of the harbour wall, though the lighthouse hadn’t been lit in forty years and the harbour had silted up long before that. He was the only person in Wrenmouth who still wrote things down, and he wrote everything: the tides, the weather, the number of gulls on the breakwater, the price of flour, the names of every child born and every person buried, all of it in a cramped brown hand across ledgers that filled the bottom three floors of the tower like sediment.

“Cracked?” he said, when she’d finished gasping out the story. “Cracked how? Cracked like a pane, or cracked like an egg?”

“Like… I don’t know. Like it opened.”

He was already moving, pulling books from the shelves faster than she would’ve thought a man of his age could move, muttering dates under his breath. “Blue, blue, she said blue, of course she did, it’s always the blue one when it matters.” He found it at last, a slim volume bound in cloth the colour of a bruise, and tucked it under his arm. “Come on, then. Don’t just stand there gawping. Haven’t you ever seen an old man hurry?”

“Not really,” said Ilse, honestly, and to her surprise he laughed, a short bark of a laugh that echoed up the stairwell.

By the time they got back the whole village seemed to have gathered at the orchard wall. Nobody had sent for them; nobody needed to. The hum had changed. It was no longer a chord but a single unbroken note, high and thin and getting higher, and the children had their hands pressed over their ears while their parents stood very still and pretended not to be frightened.

Mrs Abernathy from the bakery grabbed Ilse’s sleeve. “Is it true? Is it the old tree? Is it your grandfather’s?”

“I… yes. I think so.”

“Well, I don’t like it,” Mrs Abernathy declared, to nobody in particular. “I don’t like it one bit. Forty-two years I’ve lived here and I’ve never heard it sing like that, and I don’t mind telling you it’s putting my teeth on edge.”

Tomas pushed through the crowd without a word, and Ilse followed in his wake. Her grandmother was where they’d left her, kneeling now, both hands flat on the grass on either side of the split trunk. The silver light had spread. It ran in fine threads across the ground like water finding the cracks in a floor, and where it touched the roots of the neighbouring trees they too had begun to glow.

“How long?” said Tomas quietly.

“Since dawn. Perhaps before.” Her grandmother didn’t look up. “Read it to me. The part about the first winter.”

He opened the ledger, licked his thumb, turned the pages. When he found the place he cleared his throat, and his voice, when it came, was not his usual dry grumble but something careful and almost reverent, as though he were reading aloud in church: “In the first winter the orchard was not glass but wood, and the people were hungry, and a woman came down from the hills with a lantern in one hand and a seed in the other, and she said: plant this, and you’ll never be hungry again, but know that what is planted must one day be harvested, and the harvest will not be fruit.”

Nobody spoke. Even the wind seemed to have stopped.

“What’s that supposed to mean?” said someone at the back, too loudly. “Harvest of what?”

“It means,” said Ilse’s grandmother, rising stiffly to her feet, “that we’ve been living on borrowed light for three hundred years, and the lender has come to collect.” She turned to face the crowd, and her voice, thin as it was, carried to every one of them. “I’m sorry. I truly am. I should have told you all a long time ago. I was afraid you wouldn’t believe me… and then I was afraid you would.”

There was a terrible silence, and then everyone began talking at once.

Ilse didn’t hear most of it. She was looking at the tree. In the heart of the split, where the glass was thickest and the light was brightest, something was moving: something small and round and folded in on itself, turning slowly, like a sleeper about to wake.

“Gran,” she whispered. “Gran, there’s something inside.”

Her grandmother turned, and saw, and for the first time in Ilse’s whole life the old woman’s face went completely, utterly white.

“Get back,” she breathed. “Everyone, get back from the trees. Now. Now!”

The crowd broke and stumbled backward, tripping over one another, and the note from the orchard rose and rose until it was no longer a sound at all but a pressure, a tightening in the chest, and then, with a crack like the ice going out on a frozen river, the tallest tree split the rest of the way open and a seed the size of a man’s fist rolled out onto the glowing grass and lay there, perfectly still, pulsing gently with a warm gold light.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then the seed opened one eye. It was the exact same grey as Ilse’s own, and it looked straight at her, and Ilse, who had been frightened of nothing in particular for most of her life and of everything at once for the last ten minutes, found to her astonishment that she wasn’t frightened at all. She felt, instead, the way she had felt on the first warm day of every spring she could remember: as if something long expected had finally, quietly, arrived.

“Hello,” she said.

Behind her, very softly, her grandmother began to cry.
